# Thomas in the East: the Gospel of Thomas, the Gondophares strand, and the limits of the Chinese record

Author: Atharva Chourey
Affiliation: Atharva Chourey Lab (independent research lab)

*Manuscript in preparation — not yet peer-reviewed.*

## Abstract

This paper asks three dated questions about Thomas in the East: when was the Gospel of Thomas composed, does the Chinese record date anything about Thomas himself, and what does the dated evidence establish about a Thomas mission to India? The Gospel of Thomas is dated to c. 70–140 CE for the collection substantially as we have it, over a sayings core plausibly reaching c. 50–100, forced from above by the Greek papyri — P.Oxy 1 (shortly after 200), P.Oxy 654 and P.Oxy 655 (3rd century), and P.Oxy 5575 (published 2023), in which Thomas material is already interwoven with Matthew and Luke by the late 2nd century — and by Hippolytus (c. 222–235). The Chinese record gives NO BITE on Thomas himself: the Xi'an Stele (781) never names him, and the Zunjing catalogue's 35 titles contain no Thomas text, a verified absence across the surviving Jingjiao, Chinese and Turfan Manichaean, and Bulayïq remains, while the tradition's carrier, the Church of the East, is firmly attested in China (Alopen 635, edict 638, renaming 745, stele 781). For India, the Northwest strand is graded PLAUSIBLE–UNPROVEN: the Acts of Thomas (Syriac, c. 200–250) names King Gudnaphar and his brother Gad, and Gondophares (r. c. 20–46) appears in no Greek or Roman literary source, the Acts remaining the only literary witness to him until 1854. The South Indian community is ATTESTED from c. 550, with a Thomas tomb-cult in India by c. 590 and the tomb fixed at Mylapore by c. 1298, but the Malabar itinerary in its received form is first written in the 16th century. The community anchors are FIRM — a Persian-linked church by c. 550 and a chartered church at Kollam in 849/850 — and silent on the founder.

*Keywords:* Gospel of Thomas; Acts of Thomas; Gondophares; Jingjiao; Xi'an Stele; Zunjing; Malabar; Mylapore; Church of the East

## 1. Introduction: a hypothesis tested against dated records

The hypothesis under test in this paper is the lab's own stated position, tested — not assumed, not dismissed: *Thomas the apostle came to India.*

The method is the one used across this programme: foreign dated records as sheet-anchors for traditions that do not date themselves; verdicts graded; negatives published. A tradition may be old and still leave a late paper trail, and a paper trail is what a dating instrument can grade. Throughout, the composition of a text, the transmission of a tradition, and the events a tradition claims are kept as three separate things, because the dated evidence bears on them very differently.

Verdicts use a fixed key. **ATTESTED** means a dated record directly establishes the claim. **CORROBORATED** means independent records converge. **PLAUSIBLE–UNPROVEN** means the claim is consistent with all dated evidence and established by none. **LATE** means the claim's first written form is centuries younger than the claim. **NO BITE** means the dated record does not touch the claim at all.

Three questions organise the paper. First, what actually dates the Gospel of Thomas — the physical witnesses, the patristic mentions, and the composition debate mapped honestly, early, mainstream and late (Section 2). Second, can Chinese dating bite anywhere on Thomas — on the Xi'an Stele, in the Jingjiao corpus and its own catalogue, along the Manichaean route, or in the Tang institutional record (Section 3)? Third, the India hypothesis is graded strand by strand: the Northwest and Gondophares, the South and Malabar–Mylapore, and the community anchors that stand regardless of the founder question (Section 4). Section 5 then states the strongest evidence for and against the India mission, each at full strength, before Section 6 gives the verdict summary and Section 7 lists the anchors this work contributes.

The headline results can be stated at the outset. The Gospel's composition interval is c. 70–140 CE, and any date after c. 200 is excluded by the papyri. The Chinese record dates the Thomas tradition's eastern carrier with precision — arrival 635, edict 638, stele 781 — and contains no Thomas text, no Thomas attribution, and no mention of Thomas at all in any surviving Chinese Christian or Manichaean document; on the 1st century it has NO BITE. For India, the Northwest strand is the strongest and is graded PLAUSIBLE–UNPROVEN; the South Indian community is ATTESTED from c. 550 while its 52 CE itinerary is LATE; and the community anchors are FIRM for a Persian-linked church in India by c. 550 and a chartered church at Kollam by 849, and silent on the founder.

## 2. The Gospel of Thomas: what actually dates it

### 2.1 The physical witnesses

**Table 1. The physical witnesses to the Gospel of Thomas.**

| Witness | Content | Date | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| P.Oxy 5575 (publ. Aug 2023, *Oxyrhynchus Papyri* LXXXVII) | A syncretistic sayings fragment interweaving Matthew 6 / Luke 12 material with Thomas 27 ("fast from the world…") and probably Thomas 63 | late 2nd / early 3rd c. (editors Wallace, Fish, Holmes, independently) | Palaeography |
| P.Oxy 1 | Logia 26–33 + 77b, from a codex leaf | shortly after 200 CE | Palaeography |
| P.Oxy 655 | Logia 24, 36–39 | c. 200–250 | Palaeography |
| P.Oxy 654 | Prologue + logia 1–7, from a roll | mid-3rd c. (some: mid-to-late 3rd) | Palaeography |
| Nag Hammadi Codex II,2 | The complete Gospel, in Coptic (translation from Greek) | codex c. 340 CE | Codicology |

Three separate Greek copies of the same sayings collection were in circulation at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt in the 3rd century — the text was already a book, not a local curiosity, by then. P.Oxy 5575 pushes attested Thomasine material into the late 2nd century, and is doubly interesting: by c. 200 the Thomas sayings were already being copied interleaved with Matthew and Luke — the "separate early Thomas" picture and the "late derivative Thomas" picture both have to accommodate a copyist who treated them as one stream.

The Nag Hammadi context dates the surviving complete copy, not the composition. The codices' manufacture is independently bracketed: Codex VII's cartonnage (recycled papyrus stiffening) contains dated documents, the latest from 348 CE, giving a terminus post quem for that codex's binding; radiocarbon assay of Codex I (Lundhaug et al., 2021) returned a 99.7% range of 241–387 CE, with a combined estimate of c. 308–387. The Coptic Thomas (NHC II) sits in the same mid-4th-century production milieu.

### 2.2 Patristic mentions: termini for circulation

- **Hippolytus, *Refutation of All Heresies* 5.7.20 (c. 222–235 CE)** quotes the Naassene sect's use of a "Gospel according to Thomas," including a saying resembling logion 4 ("…find me in children of seven years… hidden in the fourteenth aeon"). This is the earliest named citation. The layering matters: Hippolytus is excerpting a Naassene source that is itself older than the *Refutation* — the Gospel was in sectarian circulation before c. 222.
- **Origen, *Homilies on Luke* 1 (c. 233 CE)** lists a "Gospel according to Thomas" among the heterodox gospels the church does not accept — bare title, no description, but proof the book was known by name in Alexandria/Caesarea.
- **Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.25.6 (c. 324 CE)** files the Gospel of Thomas among the spurious works "put forward by heretics under the names of apostles."
- **Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catecheses* 6.31 (c. 350 CE)** warns against a Gospel of Thomas he says was written by a disciple of Mani. The attribution is a conflation (see Section 3.3 on the Manichaean Thomas material), but it shows the title's currency — and its Manichaean association — by the mid-4th century.
- The Stichometry of Nicephorus assigns the Gospel 1,300 lines — a catalogue fossil of the same recognition.

What these force: a book called the Gospel of Thomas, containing sayings of the type we possess, existed and circulated by the early 3rd century at the very latest, and was already old enough to have a sectarian user-community (the Naassenes) and a heresiological reputation.

### 2.3 The composition debate, honestly mapped

**Early camp — core c. 50–100** (Valantasis: first stratum 30–60, composition 100–110; the Koester/Crossan/Pagels line). This rests on: (i) *genre* — a sayings collection with no narrative and no passion account matches the form of Q, the hypothesised sayings source behind Matthew/Luke, so Thomas could be a sibling of Q rather than a descendant of the synoptics; (ii) a substantial set of sayings whose Thomas form looks less developed than the synoptic parallel (no redactional fingerprints of Matthew or Luke); (iii) no trace of a developed church order, creed, or post-70 institutional world.

**Mainstream camp — c. 90–140** (the majority position). This rests on: (i) a minority of sayings that do appear to presuppose synoptic redaction (Thomas knows Matthew's or Luke's edited form, not just the shared tradition); (ii) the collection's editorial layering — catchword-linked clusters and a coherent ascetic theology that fit late-1st/early-2nd-century Syrian Christianity better than the 50s; (iii) the sheer time needed for a sayings tradition to be collected, titled, and ascribed to "Didymus Judas Thomas."

**Late camp — post-170** (Perrin's Diatessaron thesis; Craig Evans: not before 175–180). This rests on alleged dependence on Tatian's *Diatessaron* (c. 170): if Thomas's parallels follow Tatian's harmonised wording, Thomas must postdate the harmony. Perrin's version adds a Syriac original composed in Edessa. This remains a minority position — the Diatessaronic parallels are contested case by case — and it is squeezed hard from above by P.Oxy 5575 and P.Oxy 1: a composition c. 180 leaves very little time for the text to reach Egypt, be copied repeatedly, and be excerpted into a syncretistic florilegium by c. 200. Porter's c. 250 date is an outlier effectively falsified by the papyri — three Greek copies cannot postdate their own composition.

On language and origin, the majority holds a Greek original (the Oxyrhynchus fragments are independent Greek witnesses, not retroversions; the Coptic is a translation from Greek). A Syriac/Edessene origin is argued from the "Didymus Judas Thomas" framing (Thomas's cult centre was Edessa) and possible Semitic wordplay in individual sayings — suggestive of the tradition's Syrian home, not proof of the document's language.

### 2.4 Verdict — the Gospel

> **Composition interval: c. 70–140 CE** for the Gospel of Thomas substantially as we have it, over a sayings core plausibly reaching c. 50–100.

The interval is forced from below by the developed collection form and the sayings' engagement with the synoptic-era tradition; forced from above by P.Oxy 1 (copy shortly after 200), P.Oxy 5575 (Thomasine sayings already interwoven with Matthew/Luke by the late 2nd century), and the Naassene quotation excerpted by Hippolytus (before c. 222–235). The honest error bars: the core could be a generation earlier than 70; the collection cannot be later than c. 200 and is very unlikely to be later than c. 150.


## 3. The China sweep: can Chinese dating bite anywhere?

### 3.1 The Xi'an Stele: what it actually attests

The Xi'an Stele was erected 4 February 781 CE (Tang, Jianzhong 2 — the date is on the stone), at the Daqin temple in Chang'an; it was composed by the priest Jingjing (Syriac name: Adam), with calligraphy by Lü Xiuyan; it carries some 1,756 Chinese characters plus some seventy Syriac names of clergy; the Syriac lines name Catholicos Hnanishoʿ and Yazdbozid, a priest whose family came from Balkh. It was unearthed in 1623–1625.

The stone records, as the church's own history:

- **635 CE (Zhenguan 9):** the missionary Alopen arrives at Chang'an from Daqin (the Roman Orient) carrying scriptures and images; he is received at court, and the scriptures are translated in the imperial library.
- **638 CE (Zhenguan 12):** Taizong's edict approves the religion's propagation and authorises a temple in the Yining ward of Chang'an with twenty-one monks.
- The Messiah "left twenty-seven books" — the church's canon gesture.
- A century and a half of imperial patronage, persecution (the 845 Huichang suppression looms just after the stone), and clergy lists down to 781.

What it does not do is name Thomas. Not once. The stele's sacred history runs from creation through the Incarnation to Alopen; the apostle of the East is absent from his own church's foundation monument.

The Church of the East's own framing, from its Syriac tradition, is different and must be stated fairly: that church traced its apostolic origin to Thomas together with Addai (Thaddeus) and Mari. The Syriac *Doctrine of the Apostles* (received form c. 250) assigns "India and all its countries" to Judas Thomas; the *Didascalia* (late 3rd c.) is similar; Ephrem (d. 373) sings Thomas's Indian mission and the translation of his relics to Edessa. By the stele's century the Catholicos at Seleucia-Ctesiphon claimed jurisdiction over a metropolitan province of China. So the 635 mission understood itself as Thomas's church arriving in China — but that self-understanding is a tradition about the 1st century, recorded in the 3rd–8th, and the Chinese record dates only the arrival, never the origin.

### 3.2 The Jingjiao corpus and the Zunjing catalogue: the Thomas search

The surviving Tang-dynasty Jingjiao corpus in Chinese is small and fully catalogued: roughly seven to nine authentic documents (the Stele; the *Xuting Mishisuo Jing* / Book of Jesus-Messiah; the *Yishen Lun*, internally dated by some scholars to 642; the *Sanwei Mengdu Zan* and the *Zunjing*, both preserved in the Dunhuang manuscript Pelliot chinois 3847; the *Zhixuan Anle Jing*; the *Xuanyuan Zhiben Jing*, also carved on the Luoyang Jingjiao pillar of 814/815; the *Dasheng Tongzhen Guifa Zan*). The Alopen-era texts draw their gospel material from Matthew and Acts.

The decisive witness is the *Zunjing* ("Honoured Books," late 8th century — Jingjing's own lifetime, preserved in P.3847). It is the church's own catalogue of itself: it states that the Daqin religion possesses 530 books written on pattra leaves, of which Jingjing translated about thirty, and it lists 35 titles. In the published identifications of that list (Saeki; Li Tang, *Artifact, Text, Context*, 2020), the titles resolve to: the Psalter, the Gospels (*Ewangeliyon*), an Acts-type work, the Apostles, the Pauline epistles, Zechariah, the Law of Moses, lectionary and liturgical books (Qeryānā, Gloria/Teshbohtā, service books). Its diptych of honoured persons names prophets and apostles — David, Hosea, Peter and Paul are the examples the literature gives.

**Finding: no Gospel of Thomas, no Acts of Thomas, and no Thomas-titled or Thomas-attributed work appears among the 35 titles as identified, and no Thomas saying has been identified in any surviving Chinese Jingjiao text.** This is a verified absence: the corpus is small, the catalogue is the church's own, and the catalogue's whole purpose was to list what the church held. If a Thomas book had reached Tang China in Chinese, the Zunjing is exactly where it would have to appear.

The caveat is stated plainly: 530 claimed books, about 35 translated, a handful survive. Absence from the translated and surviving corpus is not proof that no Thomas text ever sat untranslated on a Chang'an shelf. It is proof that Thomas material formed no part of the Jingjiao church's working Chinese library — which is the claim that matters for dating anything.

### 3.3 The Manichaean route: searched, and the distinction that matters

Three different "Thomas" objects must not be conflated:

1. **The Gospel of Thomas** — the sayings gospel of Section 2.
2. **The Acts of Thomas** — the Syriac romance of Section 4.
3. **The Psalms of Thomas** — twenty psalms appended to the Coptic Manichaean Psalm-Book from Medinet Madi (ed. Allberry 1938, pp. 203–227), probably translated from an Eastern Aramaic original of the 3rd century. Their "Thomas" is genuinely contested: the apostle, Mani's disciple Thomas, or the "divine twin" figure of Syrian religion. Säve-Söderbergh (1949) showed deep Mandaean parallels; Church & Stroumsa argue for a pre-Manichaean Syrian Thomasine origin — the same milieu as the Gospel and the Acts.

The Gospel itself demonstrably circulated in Manichaean hands — in Egypt: Wolf-Peter Funk documented actual quotations from the Gospel of Thomas in Coptic Manichaica. Cyril of Jerusalem's claim (c. 350) that a disciple of Mani wrote a "Gospel of Thomas" is the patristic echo of exactly this association.

Manichaeism's eastern road is well dated: a Persian bishop (fu-duo-dan) at the Tang court in 694 (*Fozu Tongji*); the *Compendium* of Mani's teaching compiled at Xuanzong's order, internally dated 16 July 731; the Hymnscroll (S.2659, late 8th c., translated via Parthian at Turfan); the Traité fragment from Dunhuang. But no Thomas psalm, no Thomas gospel fragment, and no Thomas attribution has been identified in any Chinese or Turfan Manichaean manuscript. The Thomas material stayed in the Egyptian/Coptic branch of Manichaeism as far as all surviving evidence shows. The road existed; the Thomas cargo, on present evidence, never travelled it.

One more eastern library was checked: the Church of the East monastery at Bulayïq near Turfan yielded some 1,100 fragments (9th–13th c.) in Syriac, Sogdian and Old Turkic — Bibles, lectionaries, liturgy, and hagiography (Persian martyrs, St George, the Sleepers of Ephesus, St Pethion in Sims-Williams' 2012 catalogue register). No fragment of the Acts of Thomas has been identified in the published catalogues. The Acts was beloved in Syriac Christianity and travelled widely in the West of the church's world; its absence from the identified Turfan contents is a survival statement, not proof it never reached Central Asia — but it means Central Asia currently provides no dated witness to it either.

### 3.4 Tang records and the pre-635 question

Jingjiao temples were first officially classed as *Bosi si* ("Persian temples"); an edict of 745 (Tianbao 4) renamed them *Daqin si* ("Daqin temples") in the two capitals and all prefectures — the text is preserved in Tang sources. The *Tang Huiyao* (fasc. 49, compiled 961) carries the official account, including the 638 edict. Chang'an is fully attested (the stele's own temple; the Daqin temple site at Zhouzhi). Guangzhou: Persian and Arab merchant presence is Tang-attested (including the 758 sack of the port by Arab and Persian traders in the Tang annals); a specifically Christian church there in Tang times is claimed in later tradition but is not established by a Tang-dated document.

Pre-635, Chinese sources record Daqin embassies (166, 226, 285 CE) and more than thirty Persian embassies between 455 and 651 — diplomacy and trade, with no religious content named. No pre-635 Chinese text mentions Christians, a church, or Thomas. This was searched; it was not found. Modern claims of a Han-dynasty Thomas mission to China circulate in apologetic literature; they rest on no Chinese document and are not evidence.

Under the Mongols the Church of the East returned (the Yelikewen of Yuan records; Rabban Bar Sauma's embassy west, 1287; Mar Yaballaha III, a monk from Khanbaliq, became Catholicos in 1281). Even at this maximum eastern reach, no Thomas text in Chinese appears.

### 3.5 Verdicts — the China sweep

**Table 2. The China sweep, claim by claim.**

| Claimed connection | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Alopen's arrival 635 / edict 638 / stele 781 | ATTESTED — dated events |
| The arriving church understood itself as Thomas's church | ATTESTED as that church's tradition (Syriac sources, 3rd c. onward) — a tradition *about* Thomas, not a record *of* him |
| Gospel of Thomas in Chinese, any date | NO BITE — verified absence from the Zunjing's 35 titles and the whole surviving Jingjiao corpus |
| Thomas material via Manichaeism into China | NO BITE — Gospel-of-Thomas quotations are Coptic/Egyptian only; no Thomas item identified in Chinese/Turfan Manichaean remains |
| Acts of Thomas in Central Asia (Turfan) | NO BITE on present catalogues — no identified fragment |
| Any pre-635 Chinese mention of Christians or Thomas | NO BITE — none exists in the searched record |
| Psalms of Thomas as evidence about the apostle | LATE / not the apostle's — a 3rd-c. Manichaean-cycle psalm collection, attribution contested; do not conflate with the Gospel |

The composition-vs-transmission line is kept sharp: Chinese dates pin the Thomas tradition's carrier at 635, 638, 745, 781 and its Central Asian library at the 9th–13th centuries. Every one of those dates is a transmission date, 600–1,200 years downstream of the events the tradition claims. They do not touch the 1st century at all — and the one witness that could have carried a Thomas text east in datable form, the Zunjing catalogue, affirmatively lists a library with no Thomas in it.


## 4. The India hypothesis, strand by strand

### 4.1 Strand 1 — the Northwest: the Acts of Thomas and King Gondophares

**The text.** The *Acts of Thomas* (full title: Acts of Judas Thomas) was composed in Syriac, most plausibly in the Edessa orbit, in the early 3rd century — scholarly datings cluster c. 200–250 (Klijn's edition: the present form c. 225–250; several place the core at the turn of the century, c. 180–230). It is the earliest surviving narrative of a Thomas mission to India: Thomas, sold as a slave-carpenter to Habban, agent of King Gudnaphar (Gondophares), sails to India, builds the king a palace (in heaven, by almsgiving), and converts the royal household; a second cycle takes him to the realm of King Mazdai, where he is martyred. The *Hymn of the Pearl*, embedded in the Acts, may be an older, separate composition.

**The king is real.** Gondophares (Old Persian Vindafarnah) is an Indo-Parthian king known only from three sources: the Acts, his extensive silver and copper coinage, and one inscription. Encyclopaedia Iranica (Bivar): reign 20–46 CE over Drangiana, Arachosia and the Punjab.

**The inscription.** The Takht-i-Bahi Kharoshthi inscription is dated to year 103 of an era and Gondophares' 26th regnal year. On the standard identification of that era with the Azes era — which Bivar states is "now known to be identical" with the Vikrama era, year 1 = 57 BCE — year 103 = 46 CE, and accession falls c. 20 CE. The era assumption is stated as the method requires: the inscription does not name its era. If the era is not Azes/Vikrama, the absolute date moves with it. Robert Senior's alternative numismatic chronology further argues the inscription's king may be a later Gondophares than the coin-king of c. 20–46. The standard reading (46 CE) is the majority position, not a certainty.

**The core question, put at full strength for the mission side:** the Acts does not merely name Gondophares. It names his brother Gad as a royal figure who dies and is raised — and "Gadana/Gad" appears among the subordinate names on Gondophares' own coinage (Iranica). Gondophares appears in no Greek or Roman literary source — no historian, geographer or chronicle available to a 3rd-century Edessene writer preserves his name. Until Cunningham's coin reports of 1854, the Acts of Thomas was the only literary witness on earth to this king's existence. A romancer inventing a setting reached, somehow, for a real king of exactly the right region (the Indo-Parthian northwest), of exactly the right generation (a contemporary of the apostolic age), with a correctly named brother — for a king whom classical literature had entirely forgotten and only his coins remembered.

**The strongest counter-argument, also at full strength:** those coins are precisely the problem. Gondophares' coinage was struck in quantity and circulated across exactly the trade world — Gandhara, the Punjab, the routes to Mesopotamia — that an Edessene author inhabited; coins carry their legends for generations. Worse for the identification, "Gondophares" became a dynastic title: Gondophares IV Sases (mid-to-late 1st c.) assumed the name as supreme Indo-Parthian rulers did, as "Augustus" and "Caesar" outlived their first bearers — so the name remained current on money for a century and more after 46 CE. An author c. 220 could have taken the name (and a brother's name from the same coin legends) from the monetary landscape without any mission tradition behind it. Add the genre prior: the Acts is a romance containing talking animals and serial resurrections, and its second half abandons Gondophares entirely for the unhistorical Mazdai. And the earliest "division of the world" tradition cuts the other way — Origen (via Eusebius, *HE* 3.1) allots Parthia to Thomas and India to Bartholomew.

**Assessment.** The double hit (king + brother, right place, right generation, name absent from all classical literature) is more than a romancer needed and more than pure invention usually manages; the coin-circulation route explains the name's availability but not obviously the pairing and placement. What the strand proves: a Syriac tradition placing Thomas at Gondophares' court existed by c. 200–250 and was built around genuine 1st-century prosopography. What it cannot prove: that the meeting happened.

> **Strand 1 grade: PLAUSIBLE–UNPROVEN** — the strongest strand in the file. *Would raise it:* any pre-200 CE document or inscription independent of the Acts — Indian, Parthian or Syriac — naming Christians, or Thomas, in Gondophares' realm; a 1st–2nd-century Christian artefact in Gandhara. *Would lower it:* a pre-Acts written source (merchant handbook, onomasticon) circulating "Gudnaphar and Gad" as stock eastern-king names in Mesopotamia.

### 4.2 Strand 2 — the South: Malabar and Mylapore

**The tradition's own claim:** Thomas landed at Muziris (Kodungallur/Cranganore) in 52 CE, founded seven churches on the Malabar coast, crossed to the Coromandel coast, and was martyred at Mylapore in 72 CE, where his tomb is venerated to this day.

**First attestations in writing, in order** — the ladder matters more than any rung:

1. **c. 180–190 — Eusebius, *HE* 5.10:** Pantaenus finds Christians in "India" who say Bartholomew left them a Hebrew Matthew. The apostle is Bartholomew, not Thomas, and "India" in this period can denote South Arabia. Christians somewhere east — nothing more.
2. **c. 200–250 — the Acts of Thomas:** Thomas in "India" — but the Acts' geography is the Indo-Parthian northwest plus Mazdai's unnamed kingdom. It names no Malabar place, no Muziris, no Mylapore.
3. **c. 250 — *Doctrine of the Apostles* / *Didascalia*:** the Syriac allotment tradition now assigns India to Thomas. The assignment is 3rd-century; it is about India generally.
4. **before 373 — Ephrem the Syrian's hymns:** Thomas evangelised India, was martyred there, and his relics were brought to Edessa (the merchant Habban motif). The earliest clear Thomas–India–martyrdom complex outside the Acts itself. Still no southern itinerary.
5. **384 — Egeria's pilgrimage diary:** Thomas's martyrium at Edessa, relics present. Confirms the Edessa cult, not the Indian site.
6. **c. 547–550 — Cosmas Indicopleustes, *Christian Topography*:** a church "in the country called Male, where the pepper grows" (the Malabar coast), a bishop at Kalliana (Kalyan) "appointed from Persia," a church with Persian-appointed clergy in Taprobane (Sri Lanka). Christians in south India: ATTESTED — as a Persian-linked church. Thomas is not named as founder.
7. **c. 590 — Gregory of Tours, *Glory of the Martyrs*:** Thomas was martyred in India, where a monastery and temple mark the cult site; his remains were later translated to Edessa. First Latin witness to a Thomas cult site in India — the place is not named.
8. **6th–10th c. — the "St Thomas crosses":** stone crosses with Pahlavi inscriptions on both coasts, including the Mylapore mount cross — Persian-linked Christian presence on the Coromandel coast in the early medieval centuries.
9. **883 — the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle:** King Alfred sends Sigehelm and Æthelstan with alms "to Rome — and also to India, to St Thomas and St Bartholomew." William of Malmesbury (12th c.) adds that Sighelm returned with gems and spices. The Indian Thomas shrine was famous enough in 9th-century Wessex to be the object of a royal vow. It attests the shrine's fame, at one remove.
10. **c. 1292–1298 — Marco Polo:** the tomb of St Thomas in Maabar (the Coromandel coast), in a small town identifiable as Mylapore, venerated by Christians and Saracens alike; pilgrims carry away earth from the martyrdom spot as medicine. First unambiguous written pinning of the tomb to Mylapore.
11. **16th century — Portuguese records and the local tradition in written form:** the full itinerary — 52 CE landing at Muziris, the seven churches, the 72 CE Mylapore martyrdom — is written down for the first time, by Portuguese observers recording what the St Thomas Christians told them, and in local song-cycles (Margam Kali, Thomma Parvam) whose surviving written forms are 17th–18th-century. The tradition may be far older than its paper trail. The paper trail is what a dating instrument can grade, and it begins here.
12. **Thomas of Cana (Knai Thoma):** the tradition of an East Syriac merchant-bishop arriving with settlers, dated by the community to 345 CE or to the 8th century. No contemporary evidence; graded as legend possibly preserving a kernel of a real merchant migration.

**Trade-route plausibility — filed separately, as required.** A 1st-century sea passage to Malabar was not merely possible but routine: the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* (mid-1st c.) describes Muziris in detail — ships from Arabia and the Greek world, imports headed by "a great quantity of coin"; Pliny calls Muziris the first emporium of India and complains that India, China and Arabia drain 100 million sesterces a year from the empire; the monsoon run from the Red Sea took roughly forty days; hoards of Augustan and Tiberian gold and silver coins cluster in the Muziris hinterland and across Tamil Nadu; excavations at Pattanam (the leading Muziris candidate, near Kodungallur) have produced Roman amphorae, rouletted ware, Mediterranean glass and an intaglio. A traveller from the Roman Orient could reach Muziris in 52 CE as easily as a pepper merchant did — and this proves exactly nothing about whether one did. Routine possibility is not an attestation, and this paper does not spend it as one.

> **Strand 2 grade: SPLIT.** The south-Indian Christian community: **ATTESTED from c. 550** (Cosmas), with a Thomas tomb-cult in India by c. 590 (Gregory) and the tomb fixed at Mylapore by c. 1298 (Polo). The 52 CE / 72 CE itinerary: **LATE** — no written attestation before the 16th century; between the claim (52 CE) and its first written form stand roughly 1,450 years of silence in every Indian, Persian, Greek and Syriac source, in a region whose copper-plate habit recorded far smaller things. *Would raise it:* a single pre-Portuguese Indian document — inscription, plate, coin legend — naming Thomas; a datable 1st–3rd-century Christian artefact on the Malabar coast. *Would lower it:* demonstration that the Mylapore cult site postdates the Portuguese arrival (currently the opposite is the scholarly default — Polo precedes the Portuguese by two centuries).

### 4.3 Strand 3 — the community anchors

What the dated evidence establishes, and only that:

- **c. 550 (Cosmas):** an organised church in India — clergy, bishops ordained in Persia, congregations on the Malabar coast, at Kalyan, in Sri Lanka and Socotra. The Indian church's supply line ran to Persia, not to any local apostolic succession Cosmas mentions.
- **c. 825 (tradition) / 849 (document): Mar Sabor and Mar Proth.** Kerala tradition brings two East Syriac bishops, Sabor and Proth, to Kollam c. 823–825 with a merchant migration. The document is the Quilon (Tarisapalli) copper plates of 849/850: Ayyan Adikal, chief of Kollam, grants land and privileges to the Tarisapalli church under the merchant Maruvan Sapir Iso, in the 5th regnal year of the Chera king Sthanu Ravi; the witness plate carries signatures in Pahlavi, Kufic Arabic and Judeo-Persian (the Pahlavi signatures edited by Cereti, 2009). Precision, because documents are graded, not traditions: the plates name Maruvan Sapir Iso; the identification of the migration's leaders with "Mar Sabor and Mar Proth" is the community's tradition, consistent with the plates but not stated in them.
- **What these prove:** an East Syriac-linked, Persian-clerried, royally chartered Christian church existed in India by the mid-6th century and on the Malabar coast by 849 at the latest. **What they do not prove:** anything about the church's founder. A church attested in 550 is fully consistent with a 1st-century foundation, a 3rd-century merchant-chaplaincy origin, or a 4th–5th-century Persian mission — the anchors date the institution, and the institution's own foundation story is Strand 2's late-attested tradition, not Strand 3's evidence.

> **Strand 3 grade: FIRM** — for the proposition "a Persian-linked church existed in India by c. 550 and at Kollam by 849." It is the floor under the whole file: whatever happened in the 1st century, something was there by the 6th, and it faced Persia.


## 5. The strongest evidence, both ways

The hypothesis under test was the lab's own stated position. The grading in Section 4 is therefore restated here as an explicit balance, with each side at full strength and neither side softened.

**For a Thomas mission to India, at full strength.** The earliest surviving narrative of such a mission, the Syriac Acts of Thomas (c. 200–250), is built around genuine 1st-century prosopography that was otherwise lost to literature: it names King Gudnaphar (Gondophares, r. c. 20–46) *and* his brother Gad, who also appears on Gondophares' coinage; Gondophares appears in no Greek or Roman literary source, and until 1854 the Acts was the only literary witness to him. The king is of exactly the right region — the Indo-Parthian northwest — and exactly the right generation. The Syriac allotment tradition (c. 250) assigns India to Thomas, and Ephrem, before 373, already sings the full complex: Thomas evangelised India, was martyred there, and his relics were translated to Edessa. A Thomas tomb-cult in India is attested by c. 590 and the tomb is fixed at Mylapore by c. 1298, two centuries before the Portuguese. The community itself is real and early: a Persian-linked church in south India by c. 550 and a chartered church at Kollam by 849/850. And the journey itself was routine: the monsoon route to Muziris was a forty-day commercial run in the 1st century, described in the *Periplus* and visible in the coin hoards and the Pattanam finds. Nothing in the dated record contradicts a 1st-century mission; the Northwest strand is consistent with all of it.

**Against a Thomas mission to India, at full strength.** The earliest "division of the world" tradition contradicts the assignment: Origen's allotment gives Thomas Parthia and India to Bartholomew, and the earliest report of Christians in "India" (Pantaenus, c. 180–190) likewise names Bartholomew, not Thomas — with "India" itself ambiguous in the period. The Acts is a 3rd-century romance, written some 150–200 years after the events it claims, containing talking animals and serial resurrections, and its second half abandons the historical Gondophares for the unhistorical Mazdai. Its one hard datum — the king's name — was available without any mission tradition: Gondophares' coins circulated in quantity across the author's own trade world for generations, and "Gondophares" became a dynastic title that remained current on money for a century and more after 46 CE. The southern form of the claim is far weaker than the northern: the Acts names no Malabar place, no Muziris and no Mylapore, and the received itinerary — landing in 52 CE, seven churches, martyrdom in 72 CE — is first written in the 16th century, after roughly 1,450 years of silence in every Indian, Persian, Greek and Syriac source. The firm anchors prove an institution, not a founder: the church Cosmas saw in c. 550 was supplied and staffed from Persia, which is equally consistent with a later Persian mission or merchant chaplaincy. And the Chinese record, which dates the tradition's carrier precisely from 635, contains no Thomas at all. Nothing in the dated record establishes the mission; the silence is long, wide, and in the places — catalogues, copper plates, foundation monuments — where a record would be expected if one had been kept.

**What the balance shows.** The two statements are not symmetrical in kind. The case for rests on one strand of genuine, unexplained-by-invention prosopography inside a late romance, plus a community and a tomb-cult attested centuries later. The case against rests on the earliness of a competing allotment and on silence — early silence about the assignment, and very long silence about the southern itinerary. That is why the grades differ by strand rather than collapsing into a single verdict on "Thomas in India": PLAUSIBLE–UNPROVEN for the Northwest, ATTESTED for the southern community from c. 550, LATE for its 52 CE itinerary, and FIRM for the anchors, which are silent on the founder.

The composition-vs-transmission distinction is the paper's through-line. The Gospel can be dated, within a lifetime's width, by papyri and patristic termini. The tradition's carrier can be dated, to the year, by Chinese records. The events the tradition claims — a 1st-century mission to India — are dated by neither: they rest on a 3rd-century Syriac romance whose one checkable king checks out, and on a southern itinerary whose paper trail begins in the 16th century. Both halves of that sentence are the result, and neither is published without the other.
